15Calibration and decisions

An operator uses BLIS to choose a deployment: a configuration for the GPUs they have, or the hardware to buy. This section describes two stages of the substrate that turn simulated points into that choice. The calibration stage maps each simulated point to a distribution over what would be measured. The decision stage takes the candidate points of one decision and returns which of them are on the Pareto front, and with what probability one candidate dominates another. Both stages are fitted to measured runs and ship as data, as coefficients do. Neither changes how a step is simulated.

15.1 Which errors change a decision

A decision case is the set of points an operator chooses among: one model, workload and chip, with several configurations, each at several concurrencies. A point has three objectives: throughput per dollar, interactivity (1000 over the P90 inter-token latency in milliseconds) and the P90 time to first token. A point dominates another on a set of objectives when it is better on each of them by more than a factor 1 + ε, where ε is the tolerance recorded in the stage’s artifact (DEC-5). The front of a case is the set of its points that no point dominates, on each of the three two-objective sets and on all three objectives.

Write a simulated point’s log error on each objective as a shared error, the mean of that error over the case’s points, plus a separating error, the remainder. A shared error leaves every dominance relation, and so every front, unchanged, because it adds the same amount to both sides of each comparison. A separating error changes a pair’s relation only when it exceeds the margin by which the pair’s predicted relation holds. Point error, the measure of §11, counts both kinds alike. The two stages below therefore act on differences between candidates, and the dashboard grades decisions separately from points (§15.6).

15.2 The calibration stage

The calibration stage (substrate/calibration/) maps the simulator’s log prediction of each objective at each point to a predictive distribution. For objective k at point z of a configuration of layout kind κ, the measured value is modelled as a level times the case mean of the prediction, plus a gap scale for κ times the prediction’s deviation from that mean, plus the effect of errors in the coefficient families acting through the simulator’s sensitivity to each family, plus a discrepancy that is a Gaussian process over configuration and workload features, plus noise. The level absorbs shared error; the gap scale says how far the simulator’s differences between candidates can be trusted. The discrepancy has zero mean, so that away from the data the stage returns the simulator’s prediction, rescaled.

The fitted errors of the coefficient families are corrections to coefficients. Where they are stable across the fitting folds, the registry’s writer turns them into entries of method calibrated (§9.2), and the next fit starts from those entries. The calibration stage never edits the registry itself.

A point the simulator does not simulate, because no engine module models its layout, model or setting, has no prediction. The calibration stage then predicts it from the discrepancy alone, an interpolation between measured configurations. Such a point is an estimated point, not a simulated one, and is always labelled so (DEC-3).

15.3 The decision stage

The decision stage (substrate/decision/) gives, for an ordered pair of points of one case and one front, the probability that the first dominates the second. Its inputs are differences between the two points: the simulator’s predicted differences of the three objectives, the calibration stage’s mean differences and spreads, and the differences of the two configurations’ parallel degrees, GPU counts, engine settings and concurrencies, together with properties of the case such as model size and chip figures. No model or chip identifier is an input. Because the simulator and calibration inputs are differences, a shared error cancels before it reaches the stage.

The stage is an ensemble of gradient-boosted trees, one per front. It is monotone in each predicted difference: a larger predicted advantage of the first point never lowers the probability that it dominates. It is trained on both orderings of every pair, and its two estimates for a pair are normalised so that no two points dominate each other and the answer does not depend on the order in which the pair is presented. A point is on the predicted front unless some other point dominates it with probability above one half.

15.4 Fitting the stages

Both stages are fitted offline by writer scripts in their directories, in the way coefficients are (§9). A fit reads only training data in the sense of §10.2: units with the role fit to fit, units with the role select to choose among candidate forms, settings and inputs, and units with the role validate to report typical error. It never reads a held-out unit.

The decision stage reads calibration features. If the calibration features of a training case came from a calibration fitted on that case, they would be more accurate than the features of any new case, and the decision stage would weight them too heavily. The calibration features of every training case are therefore cross-fitted: the model–chip groups of the training data are divided into parts, and each case’s features come from a fit that excluded its part (DEC-2).

A fit writes its stage’s artifact: the fitted parameters, the training data’s content hash and role counts, the forms and settings the select units chose, and the writer’s commit. An artifact is data in the sense of §3.3: it is embedded in the release and may be replaced by a path, so a candidate artifact can be judged before it lands.

15.5 Commands

The simulator’s commands blis run and blis replay are unchanged: they simulate. Two uses of the stages are added.

  • Calibrated output. When asked, the Result document carries, beside each simulated value, the calibration stage’s predictive mean and interval, and names the artifact that produced them.
  • blis front. Given the Result documents of the candidates of one or more decision cases, it returns each point’s predicted membership of each front, the probability that it is on each front, and, for any requested pair, the probability that one dominates the other. Estimated points are included and labelled.

Search over which candidates to simulate belongs to the applications that call these commands (§5.5). The stages know nothing of how candidates were chosen.

15.6 How decisions are graded

The dashboard grades decisions as well as points, with its scoring harness and on held-out units only (§11.7). For each decision case it reports three decision grades:

  • Front F1: the precision, recall and F1 of the predicted front against the measured one, on each front, with a point that is not answered counted as predicted off the front, beside the F1 of the rule that calls every point a front point.
  • Front distance: the largest amount, in log units on the worst objective, by which a measured front point beats every predicted front point at their measured values.
  • Configuration order: over the pairs of points of different configurations at the same concurrency of which one dominates, the share for which the dominating point is named.

The grades are reported for the simulator alone, with the calibration stage, and with both stages, so that each stage’s contribution is visible. An estimated point is graded with the others and also reported apart.

15.7 Invariants

  • DEC-1 The calibration and decision stages change no simulated value: a run with or without them produces the same simulated values and the same events.Checked by: the byte gate (SIM-4) on runs with and without calibrated output.
  • DEC-2 A fit of either stage reads only training data, and the calibration features of each training case of the decision stage come from a fit that excluded that case’s model–chip group.Checked by: the writer’s role check against the ledger; a test that a held-out unit in its input makes the writer fail; a test that the cross-fitting parts are disjoint by model–chip group.
  • DEC-3 Every value the stages return for a point that was not simulated is labelled estimated, in the Result, in blis front’s output and in every grade that counts it.Checked by: schema validation and a test on a scenario the engine modules refuse.
  • DEC-4 The decision stage’s inputs from the simulator and the calibration stage are differences between the two points of a pair, so one constant per objective added to every point of a case leaves its output unchanged; it is monotone in each predicted difference; and, for every pair, its probabilities that each point dominates the other sum to at most one and do not depend on the order of the pair.Checked by: property tests over random case offsets and random pairs of the training data.
  • DEC-5 Each stage ships as one artifact per release that records its training data’s content hash and role counts, the forms and settings chosen, the dominance tolerance ε and the writer’s commit; the stage, the dashboard’s decision grades and blis front use that one ε; and a new artifact lands only with its held-out decision grades from the scoring harness, against those of the artifact it replaces and of the simulator alone.Checked by: the writer’s --check and the validators; the dashboard’s test that it grades with the artifact’s ε; the landing gate (VAL-6), extended to artifacts.
